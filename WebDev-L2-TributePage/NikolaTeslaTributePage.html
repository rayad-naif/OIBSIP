🏛️ Chapter I • Curator's Overview

The Mind of "Poetical Science" & Mental Modeling

This exhibit explores how Nikola Tesla synthesized pure physics, vivid imagination, and meticulous engineering. Unlike inventors who relied on trial-and-error physical prototypes, Tesla possessed a rare gift: the ability to run full-scale mechanical and electrical simulations inside his mind before ever putting pen to paper.

⚡ Born in a Lightning Storm (1856)

According to family tradition, Tesla was born at precisely midnight between July 9 and July 10, 1856, in the village of Smiljan (Austrian Empire, present-day Croatia) during a terrifying lightning storm. The midwife remarked that the storm was an ill omen, calling him a "child of darkness." His mother, Djuka Mandić—an inventive woman who created craft tools for household tasks—replied: "No, he will be a child of light."

From childhood, Tesla suffered from intense flashes of light accompanied by vivid visual imagery. Rather than a disability, he harnessed this ability to build machines mentally. He would run a mental turbine for weeks, inspect it for mechanical friction in his head, and then manufacture the real parts with zero dimensional errors.

🔄 The Budapest Epiphany & The War of the Currents

In February 1882, while recovering from an illness in Budapest, Tesla was taking an afternoon walk through the city park with his friend Anthony Szigeti. While reciting Goethe's Faust, a flash of insight struck him. He drew a diagram in the dirt with a wooden walking stick: the principle of the Rotating Magnetic Field.

Arriving in New York in June 1884 with four cents in his pocket, Tesla briefly worked for Thomas Edison. Edison promised $50,000 for redesigning inefficient DC dynamos, but when Tesla completed the task, Edison dismissed it as "American humor." Tesla resigned immediately, igniting the bitter "War of the Currents." Partnering with industrialist George Westinghouse, Tesla proved that polyphase Alternating Current (AC) could be transmitted over vast distances at high voltage—a feat impossible with Edison's low-voltage DC grid.

📡 High Frequency, Wireless Vision & Wardenclyffe

Following his triumph at the 1893 Chicago World's Fair and the 1895 Niagara Falls hydroelectric project, Tesla pushed into unexplored territory: high-frequency currents and wireless energy. In 1899 at Colorado Springs, he built a giant high-voltage oscillator capable of producing artificial lightning bolts over 100 feet long.

He envisioned a global wireless system that would transmit not only news, stock updates, and pictures, but also electrical power directly through the Earth's upper atmosphere. His massive Wardenclyffe Tower project on Long Island ultimately failed due to financial pullouts by J.P. Morgan, yet his foundational patents laid the bedrock for modern radio, wireless telemetry, radar, and remote control.

Personal Quirks & Peculiarities

  • • Obsession with 3-6-9: Tesla insisted on walking around a block 3 times before entering a building and required 18 napkins to clean his table settings.
  • • Photographic Memory: He possessed a eidetic memory, memorizing entire books and technical blueprints effortlessly.
  • • Love for Pigeons: In his final years living at the Hotel New Yorker, he spent hours feeding street pigeons and famously cared for a special white pigeon.

Foundational Engineering Pillars

1. Polyphase AC Motor Eliminated graphite brushes, friction wear, and dangerous sparking commutator hazards.
2. Step-Up Transformer Allowed safe low-voltage generation to step up to high voltage for efficient long-distance grid lines.
3. High-Frequency Tesla Coil Resonant transformer circuit designed to produce high-voltage, low-current, high-frequency electricity.
⚙️ Chapter II • Interactive Physics Workshop

Simulating the Polyphase AC Rotating Magnetic Field

Prior to Tesla, direct current (DC) motors required mechanical graphite brushes physically rubbing against a spinning copper commutator to flip polarity. Tesla realized that by feeding two out-of-phase Alternating Currents ($90^\circ$ phase angle) into stationary outer coils (Stator), a smoothly spinning magnetic vector is created in thin air without any mechanical contact.

Historical Significance: Because Tesla's motor relied on induction (creating current in the inner rotor purely via the moving magnetic field of the outer stator), it eliminated spark hazards entirely. This enabled high-power electric motors to run continuously in hazardous factories, mines, and electric transit systems.
